Some Questions Need
Answers From Within

In an age where artificial intelligence can answer almost anything, are we forgetting how to ask — and live with — the questions that matter most?

“The danger isn't that AI answers too many questions. The danger is that we stop asking ourselves the ones only we can answer.” — on the temptation of the instant answer

Every breakthrough began
as a question, not an answer

None of these thinkers rushed toward certainty. They let curiosity mature into understanding — sometimes over years.

c. 400 BCE — Athens
Socrates
“What is a good life?” — a question he never stopped asking, even at the end.
He questioned what it meant to live well right up until the end of his life — never landing on a final answer, and never treating that as a failure.
1666 — Woolsthorpe
Isaac Newton
“Why did the apple fall?” — wondering why it fell down, not drifted upward, led somewhere no one had gone.
A simple observation, held onto and turned over, became the seed of a theory that redefined how we understand motion itself.
1896 — Paris
Marie Curie
“What is this mysterious radiation?” — a question pursued long before it had a name.
She chased an unnamed phenomenon with patient, methodical curiosity — long before the word "radioactivity" existed to describe it.
1905 — Bern
Albert Einstein
“What would happen if I could travel alongside a beam of light?” — turned over for years before it revealed anything.
He spent years simply imagining the scenario, returning to it again and again, long before it resembled anything like an answer.
1950 — Manchester
Alan Turing
“Can machines think?” — the question that still frames the one this page is about.
Decades later, his question hasn't been settled so much as reframed — into whether machines can decide which questions are worth asking.
The difference is not the technology

It's in us

A calculator never destroyed mathematics — it freed mathematicians from repetitive arithmetic. AI can do the same for thinking, if we let it.

Used well, AI expands curiosity

  • Discovers perspectives you hadn't considered
  • Challenges assumptions you didn't know you held
  • Recommends books you'd never have found
  • Organizes ideas after you've wrestled with them
  • Frees time for the reflection that actually matters

Used carelessly, it replaces it

  • Delivers conclusions before you've formed a view
  • Skips the discomfort where understanding forms
  • Answers the question instead of sharpening it
  • Ends the search the moment it gets convenient
  • Quietly does your reflecting for you

Ask yourself first

  1. What do I already know?
    Write it down before you search. You'll often find you know more than you thought — or that you were only assuming.
  2. Why do I believe it?
    Trace it to a source: something you read, someone you trust, or something you experienced firsthand. Notice which.
  3. What evidence supports my view?
    Name one concrete piece of evidence. If nothing comes to mind, that itself is worth noticing.
  4. What evidence challenges it?
    Steelman the opposite view for a moment. Discomfort here is a sign the exercise is working.
  5. Have I thought deeply enough to ask a better question?
    Sometimes the most useful thing AI can do is sharpen your question — not answer the first one you typed.

But some questions need answers from within.

Wisdom has never belonged to those who found answers the fastest.

It belongs to those with the courage to ask difficult questions, the humility to admit they don't yet know, and the patience to discover answers through reflection.
